# Presentation notes (about 6.5 minutes, 9 slides)

Slides: `writeup/slides.pptx` (PDF export: `writeup/slides.pdf`). The same text is in each slide's notes pane. Every number also appears in the report and in the saved analysis outputs in the repository.

**1. ANNNI phase diagram under CNOT-target noise (30 s).** We mapped the ANNNI phase diagram on small periodic rings, then asked what the challenge's noise model, a depolarizing channel on the target after every CNOT, does to it. Headline: at p equals 0.01 the ferromagnet disappears under fixed thresholds, even though the boundary positions defined by derivatives barely move. At p equals 0.05 the state is essentially maximally mixed. Zero-noise extrapolation restores the p equals 0.01 diagram.

**2. Model, order parameters and reference curves (45 s).** Three competing terms: ferromagnetic nearest-neighbour coupling, frustrating next-nearest-neighbour coupling, and a transverse field. Finite rings keep the global spin-flip symmetry, so the one-point magnetisation vanishes even in ordered phases. We therefore use the structure factor at q = 0 and q = pi over 2, with the uncorrelated floor subtracted, together with m_x. The overlay curves are approximate thermodynamic fits: the lower one is a Pokrovsky-Talapov transition and the upper one KT. They are references, not labels, and our thresholds come from limiting-case anchor regions rather than from these curves.

**3. Methods and validation (45 s).** The reference is sparse exact diagonalisation; the circuit is a Hamiltonian-variational ansatz. We first tried a hardware-efficient ansatz, which could not get below 1 to 3 percent energy error near the boundaries. The HVA reaches a median energy error of 4e-5 and agrees with the exact labels at 98.9 percent of points. The cost is 128 CNOTs, so 128 noise channels. Every layer of the pipeline is validated against PennyLane to about 1e-14.

**4. Exact phase diagram and finite-size behaviour (60 s).** The ferro boundary hardly depends on N and sits on the Ising estimate; the fidelity-susceptibility peak moves towards the DMRG value. The antiphase boundary drifts down strongly with N towards the KT line, which is the expected slow, KT-like finite-size behaviour. N = 10 cannot hold a period-4 pattern and is anomalous. On the floating phase we are careful: at N = 12 the entanglement entropy peaks near the KT line, but the ordering wavevector stays locked at pi over 2, so we call it unresolved.

**5. Effect of depolarising noise at p = 0.01 and 0.05 (60 s).** At p = 0.01 the order drops to about 60 percent of its clean value, but not evenly: the ferromagnet falls to 0.37 while the antiphase keeps 0.59. With fixed thresholds the ferromagnet vanishes from the map, though recalibrating the thresholds recovers it, so it is the absolute signal that is lost, not the phase. The field where the order changes fastest barely moves, median shift 0.005. At p = 0.05 the purity is only 20 percent above that of a fully mixed state. The noise does not change the Hamiltonian; what degrades is the prepared state.

**6. Channel-insertion analysis of noise sensitivity (45 s).** We calculated the exact first-order effect of each of the 128 channels separately. The ferromagnet is 2.3 times more sensitive than end-of-circuit counting predicts, the antiphase 1.6 times: errors made early are carried through the later gates. The states themselves do not explain the gap, since one flipped qubit costs both phases the same fraction of correlators; it is circuit dynamics and depth. The first-order formula gets the direction of the shift right but underestimates its size, because p = 0.01 is already nonlinear for this circuit depth. This ranking belongs to this circuit and diagnostic; it is not a law about the phases.

**7. Circuit depth, re-optimisation and clustering (50 s).** We reran the same experiment with shallower circuits. Shallow circuits are wrong even without noise, and deep ones collect more noise, so the best depth falls as p rises. The antiphase is already accurate with two layers, while the ferromagnet needs all four. That is a second reason the ferromagnet suffers most. We also tried re-optimising the parameters at each noise level: the noisy energy improves, but only by pushing the state towards the field direction; the order parameters barely change, so the state that minimises the noisy energy is not the one that best reveals the phase. A label-free k-means clustering of the same observables reproduces our clean labels at 99 percent.

**8. Error mitigation by extrapolation and parity checks (45 s).** Zero-noise extrapolation on the correlators, never on the labels, with the clean result held out: at p = 0.01 the correlator error falls six-fold and 98 percent of labels are recovered, at the price of about 19 times more variance. At p = 0.05 Richardson fails, because the correlators decay exponentially in p and a polynomial cannot follow that. A two-point exponential model does: 97.5 percent of labels come back, but at a variance cost of about fifty thousand, so on hardware this is not free. Parity verification helps only a little, because most errors keep the parity.

**9. Conclusions and limitations (30 s).** An accurate clean diagram; the ferro side converges quickly while the antiphase side drifts towards KT. For a circuit deep enough to be right, 1 percent noise already removes absolute order, and 5 percent leaves only a weak signal: depth versus noise is the real trade-off. We also ran a quench from the fully polarised state: the Loschmidt rate peaks sharply only for quenches deep into the ordered side, but at p = 0.01 the channel error is about eight times the Trotter error, so the dynamical signature does not survive noise. Limitations: small N, approximate overlays, the fixed-parameter protocol, and no shot noise.
